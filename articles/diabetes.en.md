## What is diabetes?

Diabetes is a long-term condition in which glucose (sugar) in the blood becomes too high. One high result does **not** always mean that you have diabetes. A clinician interprets it with your symptoms and history, and may confirm it with another laboratory test.

> “My sugar is high. Does that mean I have diabetes?” Not necessarily. Do not panic over one number—but do not ignore it either.

## Glucose and insulin: a simple explanation

Glucose is an important source of energy. Foods such as rice, roti, bread, potatoes and fruit supply carbohydrates that the body digests into glucose. Insulin, a hormone made by the pancreas, helps glucose move from the blood into the body's cells.

Think of glucose as fuel and insulin as a key that helps the cells use that fuel. In diabetes, the body may not make enough insulin, may not use it effectively, or both. Glucose then builds up in the blood.

## Main types of diabetes

### Type 1 diabetes

In type 1 diabetes, the immune system damages the pancreatic cells that make insulin. People with type 1 diabetes need insulin to survive. It can develop in children, teenagers or adults; it is not simply caused by eating too much sugar.

### Type 2 diabetes

Type 2 is the most common form. The body becomes less responsive to insulin (insulin resistance), and over time the pancreas may not make enough. Family history, age, excess body weight, physical inactivity and previous prediabetes can affect risk. For people at higher risk, sustainable lifestyle changes and suitable medical care can sometimes prevent or delay type 2 diabetes.

### Gestational diabetes and other forms

Gestational diabetes develops or is first recognized during pregnancy. It can affect both mother and baby, and it increases the mother's later risk of type 2 diabetes. Pregnancy needs its **own** testing and care plan; the table below is for nonpregnant adults.

Other forms can be associated with genetic conditions, diseases of the pancreas, hormonal disorders or certain medicines such as glucocorticoids.

## What is prediabetes?

Prediabetes means blood glucose is above the normal range but below the diabetes range. It raises the risk of developing type 2 diabetes and may be associated with greater cardiovascular risk. It is a chance to act early: food choices, physical activity, weight management and, in some situations, medical treatment can lower or delay that risk.

## How is diabetes diagnosed?

Doctors use laboratory tests and clinical assessment. The following are standard diagnostic thresholds for **nonpregnant** people; the choice of test depends on the situation.

| Laboratory test | Diabetes-range result |
| --- | --- |
| Fasting plasma glucose, after at least 8 hours without calories | 126 mg/dL or higher |
| HbA1c, using an appropriately standardized laboratory method | 6.5% or higher |
| Plasma glucose 2 hours after a 75-g oral glucose tolerance test | 200 mg/dL or higher |
| Random plasma glucose **with classic symptoms** or a hyperglycemic crisis | 200 mg/dL or higher |

If there are no unmistakable symptoms or a hyperglycemic crisis, diagnosis generally requires **two abnormal results**—from the same test on different occasions or from two appropriate tests. A home glucose meter is useful for monitoring, but a single home reading is not normally enough to diagnose diabetes. HbA1c also needs careful interpretation in some medical conditions, so discuss unexpected or conflicting results with a clinician.

## Possible symptoms

Especially early in type 2 diabetes, there may be **no obvious symptoms**. Possible signs include:

- Increased thirst and frequent urination
- Increased hunger or unexplained weight loss
- Tiredness or blurred vision
- Repeated infections or slowly healing wounds
- Genital itching or fungal infections
- Tingling or numbness in the hands or feet

These symptoms can have other causes. Persistent symptoms deserve a proper medical assessment.

## Why it matters even when you feel well

High blood glucose can exist without making you feel ill. Over time, poorly controlled diabetes can injure blood vessels and nerves and increase the risk of heart disease, stroke, kidney disease, eye problems, nerve damage, foot ulcers and infections.

Managing diabetes is therefore about more than lowering a sugar number. It also means protecting your heart, kidneys, eyes, feet and overall quality of life.

## Can diabetes be cured or go into remission?

People with type 1 diabetes need insulin. Type 2 diabetes is usually treated as a chronic condition, although some people achieve **remission**. The commonly used definition is HbA1c below 6.5% for at least three months **without glucose-lowering medication**, after a previous diagnosis of type 2 diabetes. Remission is not the same as a permanent cure: follow-up and healthy habits still matter. Never stop medication simply to test whether you are in remission; make that decision with your treating clinician.

## Living well with diabetes in Nepal

Many people with diabetes live active lives with suitable treatment, regular review, good sleep, physical activity and eating patterns they can maintain. Diabetes does **not** automatically mean never eating rice, dal, roti, potatoes or fruit again.

What matters is the whole meal and your own situation: portion size, frequency, other foods on the plate, activity, waist size, blood pressure, cholesterol and medicines. A good plan should fit everyday life rather than demand that you abandon all familiar food.

## If a blood-sugar result is high

Do not make a diagnosis from one number. Arrange appropriate laboratory testing and discuss whether an HbA1c or repeat test is needed. If diabetes is confirmed, your clinician may also assess blood pressure, cholesterol, kidney function, eyes, feet and cardiovascular risk. A realistic plan for food, activity, sleep, medicines and follow-up can then be made for you.

## When to seek urgent care

Very high blood glucose together with repeated vomiting, severe abdominal pain, dehydration, rapid or deep breathing, increasing sleepiness, confusion, fainting, marked weakness or inability to keep fluids down can signal a serious metabolic emergency. **Seek immediate in-person emergency care.** Do not try to manage these symptoms using this website or by waiting for an online appointment.

## Common misunderstandings

### “Diabetes is caused only by eating sugar.”

No. Genetics, insulin resistance, body weight, activity, age, pregnancy, medicines and other factors may contribute.

### “I feel fine, so I cannot have diabetes.”

No. Type 2 diabetes can be present for years without clear symptoms.

### “If I need insulin, my diabetes must be extremely severe.”

Not necessarily. Insulin is used for different reasons, including type 1 diabetes, pregnancy, acute illness or when other treatments are insufficient.

### “People with diabetes cannot eat rice.”

Rice can be part of a suitable plan. Portion size, the rest of the meal and individual response matter.

## A few more questions

### Can younger or lean people have diabetes?

Yes. Diabetes can occur at any age and body size. Type 1 and type 2 diabetes have different causes and patterns; a clinician determines what applies to you.

### Can diabetes be present with a normal fasting result?

Sometimes another test, such as HbA1c or an oral glucose tolerance test, adds useful information. The right test depends on your symptoms and circumstances.

### Does everyone need medicine?

Treatment differs by diabetes type, glucose level and overall health. Some people improve substantially with lifestyle changes; others need medicines or insulin. Do not start, stop or adjust treatment without personal advice.

## The main message

One high reading is a reason to **understand and confirm**, not to panic. Proper testing matters because diabetes may have no symptoms. Good care aims to prevent complications and protect the whole person—not just improve a number on a report.
